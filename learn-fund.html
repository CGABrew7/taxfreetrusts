How to fund a living trust

A trust controls only what you put in it. Funding means changing the title or the beneficiary on each asset, so the trust owns it or receives it.

The home

Sign a new deed that transfers the home from you to you as trustee. Record it with the recorder or register of deeds in the county where the home sits.

  • Your mortgage. Federal law, the Garn-St Germain Act, generally stops a lender from calling the loan when you move your home into a revocable trust that you benefit from and live in.
  • Insurance. Tell your homeowner's insurer and ask your title insurer whether the policy follows the transfer.
  • Homestead. Some states want specific deed language to keep homestead protections. Homestead deeds are picky. Read the form before you record.

Bank accounts

Retitle each account in the trust's name, or add the trust as the payable on death beneficiary. Your bank has a form for both.

Brokerage accounts

Retitle the account to the trust, or add a transfer on death designation that names the trust.

Retirement accounts

Do not retitle an IRA or a 401(k). Changing the owner can count as a withdrawal and trigger income tax. Review the beneficiary form instead, and name people or the trust as your plan requires.

Life insurance

A policy pays whoever is on the beneficiary form, whatever your will or trust says. Confirm the form matches your plan.

Vehicles

Rules vary by state. Some states offer a transfer on death title for cars and trucks. Check with your state's motor vehicle office.

A shop, a farm or a business interest

Assign your interest to the trust in writing. Read the operating agreement, partnership agreement or bylaws first, since many limit transfers or require consent.

Everything else

An assignment of personal property covers furniture, tools, jewelry and the other items that carry no title.

Keep the funding list with the trust. Note what moved and the date it moved.
